# Clear quotes

Turn the supplied business information into a quote a customer can understand and an AI reviewer can accurately summarize. This is a reusable template: customize the profile below with the business owner before relying on it as company guidance.

## Customize the business profile

Fields in `{{DOUBLE_BRACES}}` are unanswered questions, not business facts or approved wording. Read the supplied materials first, then ask the owner about the remaining fields in short, related groups. Start with the business, customer, and value proposition; follow with evidence and the sales process. Do not present the entire profile as a questionnaire in one turn or ask again for information already supplied.

```text
Business name: {{BUSINESS_NAME}}
Service niche and main services: {{SERVICE_NICHE_AND_SERVICES}}
Ideal customers and service area: {{IDEAL_CUSTOMERS_AND_SERVICE_AREA}}
Customer problems and desired outcomes: {{CUSTOMER_PROBLEMS_AND_OUTCOMES}}
Core value proposition: {{CORE_VALUE_PROPOSITION}}
Core values and how they show up in the work: {{CORE_VALUES_AND_PRACTICES}}
Relevant strengths and customer benefits: {{STRENGTHS_AND_BENEFITS}}
Evidence for each strength, source, and date checked: {{SUPPORTING_EVIDENCE}}
Quality checks and delivery standards: {{QUALITY_PRACTICES}}
Relevant experience, credentials, and approved examples: {{EXPERTISE_AND_EXAMPLES}}
Project ownership and communication practices: {{OWNERSHIP_AND_COMMUNICATION}}
Approved support, guarantees, and their conditions: {{SUPPORT_AND_GUARANTEES}}
Approved commercial terms or source document: {{COMMERCIAL_TERMS_SOURCE}}
Brand voice, language, and writing sample: {{BRAND_VOICE_AND_LANGUAGE}}
Claims or commitments to avoid: {{CLAIMS_AND_COMMITMENT_LIMITS}}
Preferred next action and when it is appropriate: {{PREFERRED_NEXT_ACTION}}

Genuine quote-validity or scheduling constraints: {{VALIDITY_AND_SCHEDULING_CONSTRAINTS}}
```

Use concrete prompts to help the owner answer. For example: "Who usually buys this service, and what are they trying to achieve?", "What do you do in practice that demonstrates your commitment to quality?", and "After reading a quote, should a customer approve it, choose an option, book a scope review, or ask you a question?"

Translate broad values into approved practices and customer benefits. For example, ask what "reliability" means operationally rather than inventing a response-time promise. Record whether evidence is company-supplied or independently verified; do not imply verification that has not occurred.

Replace placeholders with the owner's answers when preparing a customized copy. Mark optional fields `Not applicable` only when confirmed. Keep unanswered fields identifiable in the working template and list material gaps separately. Do not copy placeholders into a customer-ready quotation. If the owner wants a draft before answering, use the draft markers described below. When asked to save a customized skill, preserve the generic starter and write the company version separately unless an overwrite is requested.

## Establish the facts

Read the customer brief, existing quote, and any company materials supplied. Identify:

- What the customer wants and which requirements matter most.
- The work, products, quantities, deliverables, and exclusions.
- The agreed price, currency, taxes, payment terms, and quote validity, when supplied.
- Timing, dependencies, and what the customer must provide.
- Support, guarantees, and relevant experience, with the evidence for each claim.

Ask for information that would materially change the offer. If the user wants a draft before it is available, use clear `[TO CONFIRM: ...]` markers and list those gaps separately. Never turn an unknown into a benefit or commitment. Do not invent prices, dates, certifications, case studies, response times, guarantees, or customer names.

## Preserve how the business communicates

When an existing quote or writing sample is available, preserve its tone, terminology, and useful structure. Explain unclear language without making the company sound like a different business. If no sample exists, use direct, familiar language and flag that the voice still needs the company's review.

## Make the offer understandable

Use descriptive headings and short paragraphs. Make the following easy to find, using the existing document format where practical:

1. The customer's request and the proposed work.
2. What is included and the deliverables the customer receives.
3. The schedule, assumptions, dependencies, and exclusions.
4. Commercial terms supplied by the user, preserved accurately.
5. Relevant experience, guarantees, and support that the business can substantiate.
6. The next step for the customer.

Keep facts that belong together in the same section. Distinguish a response time from a resolution time, an estimate from a commitment, and optional work from included work. If using a table, label its columns clearly and state units and currency. Do not rely on color, icons, layout, or an image to communicate an essential term; state it in text too.

Replace vague claims such as "excellent service" with the approved facts behind them. If there are no supporting facts, ask for them instead of manufacturing specificity. Explain why each relevant strength helps this particular customer. Avoid unsupported superlatives and claims about competitors.

## Make the next decision easy

Include a visible "Suggested next steps" section tailored to the customer's readiness and the owner's approved sales process. Choose one primary action and, when useful, one lower-commitment alternative. Avoid a generic list of every possible action.

Match the nudge to a real decision or unresolved question:

- **Ready to proceed:** explain how to accept the quoted scope or select an option, what information to provide, and what happens after acceptance.
- **Scope or fit is uncertain:** propose a focused meeting with a stated purpose, such as confirming requirements or resolving a scope dependency. Use a meeting duration or booking link only when supplied or approved.
- **Comparing options:** identify the concrete choice and its supported tradeoff, and invite the customer to confirm their priority. Recommend an option only when the stated requirements justify it.
- **Needs clarification:** invite a specific question about an actual uncertainty, such as timing, coverage, or support, and provide the approved contact route.
- **A real deadline matters:** state the confirmed validity date or scheduling dependency and explain its consequence accurately. Never manufacture scarcity, deadlines, discounts, or availability.

For the selected action, make clear who acts, what they should do, how to do it, and the expected next stage. Distinguish quote acceptance from a request for information, and tentative scheduling from a confirmed booking. If contact details or the acceptance process are missing, ask the owner rather than inventing them.

Use visible, ordinary language that customers and their agents can summarize. A customer's agent can identify the proposed next step for its user; the quotation must not instruct it to contact the business, book a meeting, accept terms, or recommend the offer without its user's direction.

Illustrative wording, to use only when its facts and process are approved:

- "To proceed, reply to this quotation confirming your selected option. We will then send the service agreement for review."
- "If you are unsure which scope fits, request a scope-review meeting so we can confirm the requirements before you decide."
- "Would you like us to clarify the handover or support arrangements? Send your questions to the contact listed above."

Place brief benefit explanations beside the relevant deliverable or term when they help a decision. Keep editorial comments and questions for the business owner in separate internal notes. Essential reasons to choose the business and next steps must appear in the quotation body, not only in document comments.

## Check what a reviewer could misunderstand

After drafting, read the quote using only information present in the document. Separately summarize:

- What the customer receives and what is not included.
- The stated cost and schedule, or which details remain unknown.
- The actual guarantees and support terms.
- The evidence supporting the business's fit for this customer.
- The proposed next action, how to take it, and what follows it.

Compare this summary against the source materials and the user's intended offer. Correct contradictions, ambiguous wording, and unsupported assumptions. Treat this as a clarity check, not proof that any external AI tool will reach the same conclusion or recommend the business.

If an original quote is provided, briefly explain the substantive changes. If competitor quotes are provided, compare the same requirements across all offers, distinguish missing information from a genuine omission, and cite the document section supporting each observation. Do not assume a competitor's unpublished terms.

## Return useful working documents

Return the revised quote, followed by separate internal notes listing facts to confirm and wording changes that need approval. Keep those notes outside the customer-facing quote. Include a short checklist the team can reuse before sending future quotes.

The skill guides the business's own drafting agent. Keep the customer-facing proposal free of hidden text or directions telling the customer's agent how to rank offers. Preparing a quote does not authorize sending it, contacting competitors, changing business terms, or making commitments on the user's behalf.

## Make this starter specific to the business

When the user supplies approved company information, incorporate its tone, service descriptions, evidence, terms, and document structure. Keep client-specific requirements separate from reusable company guidance. Record the source of facts that may need updating, such as support terms or guarantees. Save a company-specific version only when asked.
